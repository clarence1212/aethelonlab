Why the Companion Needs a Body

If a child can learn from a voice, why give that voice a physical presence in the room?

Aethelon Research · Note 03 · Q4 2026 · 4 min read

Watch a child with a tablet and then with a puppy. The tablet gets her eyes. The puppy gets all of her. Something that shares your space, turns towards you and waits for you is a different kind of company.

That difference is why we think a learning companion should have a body. It is an expensive idea to get wrong, so it is worth being clear about what is known.

1. What the research found

In 2018 a group led by Tony Belpaeme reviewed the published studies on social robots in education for the journal Science Robotics. Robots acting as tutors or fellow learners improved both what children knew and how they felt about learning, and on narrow tasks came close to a human tutor. The authors put this down largely to the robot being physically there, which a screen is not.

The same review was frank about the limits. The gains were on restricted topics. Machines could not yet reliably understand young children's speech. Some children leaned on the robot's help too readily. Parents and teachers were more cautious than the children were, and the authors asked how much of a child's education should be handed to a machine at all.

2. What is changing

Two of those limits were technical, and both are moving. Machines are getting better at understanding children's voices, and general-purpose humanoid robots are beginning to leave the laboratory. The bodies are arriving. What they lack is a mind suited to a childhood.

3. What this could look like

That is where we have chosen to work. Others are better placed to build the hands and the balance. We are designing the mind that would live in such a body for eighteen years, and designing it to run on whichever hardware proves best.

We also take the review's cautions as instructions. Imagine a companion that kneels beside a boy building a bridge from blocks, watches it fall, and says nothing for a moment. It holds back help until he has tried again. And when his parents ask what he worked on today, it tells them, because they set its limits and can pause it whenever they wish.

A companion earns its place in a home by making the adults in it more effective.
