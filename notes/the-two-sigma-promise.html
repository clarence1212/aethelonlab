The Two Sigma Promise: When Every Child Has a Tutor

Forty years ago a researcher showed what a personal tutor is worth, and called it a problem because no society could afford one for every child. That is changing.

Aethelon Research · Note 05 · Q4 2026 · 4 min read

For most of history, the best education money could buy was a person: a tutor who knew one child well and stayed for years. Royal households had them. Almost nobody else did.

1. What a tutor is worth

In 1984 the educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom put a number on it. Students taught one-to-one, and not moved on until they had mastered each step, finished about two standard deviations ahead of students in an ordinary class. Put plainly, the average tutored student did better than roughly 98 in 100 of the others.

Bloom did not call this a triumph. He called it a problem, because no society could pay for a tutor for every child. His challenge was to find something that worked as well and could reach everyone.

2. What has changed

For four decades the answer was partial. Then AI made patient, conversational teaching cheap. In 2025 a team at Harvard published a careful trial with 194 undergraduates in a physics course. Students who worked with a purpose-built AI tutor learned more than twice as much as those in a well-taught active class, and in less time.

The limits matter as much as the headline. These were university students, in one subject, for two weeks. The tutor was designed by their own lecturers, and it was built to make students reason instead of handing over answers. A general chatbot dropped into a classroom is a different thing.

3. What this could look like

We take two lessons. The first is that design decides the outcome. A tutor that holds back the answer and asks the next question is what produces the gain, so the companion we imagine coaches before it explains.

The second is about time. Every trial so far is short. Nobody has seen what happens when a tutor knows a learner for years: which ideas were fragile at seven, what finally made fractions make sense, how this particular mind likes to be challenged.

Imagine a world where intelligence is as available as electricity. In that world Bloom's problem stops being about cost. What remains scarce is a tutor who remembers, and that is the thing we are setting out to build.
